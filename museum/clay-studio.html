How the studio works

Three places to work. At the bench you shape clay by hand with tools. At the wheel you throw a pot. In the kiln you fire what you made. Drag on the clay to use a tool; drag anywhere else to look round; scroll to come closer. Click a piece on the shelf to bring it back to the bench.

The clay at the bench

The clay is a closed skin of about forty thousand points. After every touch its volume is measured exactly, and the clay near the tool is moved out or in by the one amount that puts the volume back. So a thumb pressed in raises a rim round itself, and squeezing one place swells another. Only the loop tool and the needle take clay away, and only “add clay” gives any; both are counted. It cannot tear or join to itself, which real clay can.

The clay on the wheel

The same model as The Potter’s Wheel: a stack of rings that conserves its clay. A still tool centres it, a moving one does not; thin wet walls give way; speed throws anything off-centre further out. When you cut the pot free it becomes a skin like any other and can be carved, glazed and dusted at the bench. Soft clay can also go the other way, from the bench to the wheel. The wheel only knows round things, so what arrives is the round pot nearest to what you made: each slice keeps its average radius, a hollow pressed in from the top becomes the inside, and anything lopsided becomes wobble to be centred. Carved detail, glaze and dust do not survive the trip, and fired clay cannot be thrown.

The kiln

The glow is the colour a hot body really has at that temperature. Below about 600 °C clay is still mud that would dissolve in water. It shrinks as it fires, by about an eighth in all at stoneware heat. Unfired glaze is a pale chalky powder whatever its final colour; it melts to glass at around 1200 °C; hotter than that it runs down the wall, and past about 1285 °C the pot itself sags. The cone numbers are the potter’s usual measure and are approximate. These thresholds are typical figures for a stoneware body, not measurements of any one clay, and real glazes each have their own range.

The gem dust

The hammer is magic; the rest is true. Diamond is the hardest thing there is, but hard is not tough: it cleaves, and a hammer does shatter it. Each blow makes the grain finer. Diamond is also pure carbon, and in the air of a kiln it burns away above roughly 700 °C. Ruby is aluminium oxide coloured by chromium and comes through any firing here. So dust a piece with diamond after its last firing, or keep the kiln cool.

Keys

At the bench: 1 to 9 choose the shaping tools, G the glaze brush, D diamond dust, R ruby dust; [ and ] make the tool smaller and larger; Ctrl+Z undoes. At the wheel everything can be done from the keyboard. 1, 2, 3 and 4 choose the outside tool, the inside tool, the thumb and steady hands. Space puts the tool on the clay and lifts it off. While it is lifted, up and down choose the height it will go on at. The arrows move the tool as you see it: left and right across, up and down the wall; with Shift it moves slowly. Minus and equals slow and quicken the wheel. W is water, C cuts the pot free, Enter takes it to the bench, N is new clay. Holding H steadies the clay for as long as it is down. B, T and K go to the bench, the wheel and the kiln.

Taking your work away

“Save a photo” gives a JPG of the view you have. “Export 3D” gives a ZIP holding a Wavefront OBJ: a watertight mesh in millimetres with a colour on every point, ready for a slicer, Blender or a game engine. The ZIP is there because this page is not allowed to hand over a bare .obj file. Glitter and gloss are not in the OBJ; they are properties of light, not of shape.
