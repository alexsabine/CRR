About this bench

One small creature and one hidden thing. Nothing here is a loop of drawings: every hill, spring, pile and number is worked out afresh for each picture from the small model below. Free energy is measured in nats, the natural unit of information; one nat is about 1.44 bits.

The model

The hidden thing is x, the distance from the creature's skin to the lamp, in hands. The creature never touches x. It touches only its skin (the feeling) and its feet (the rail). That pair is what the theory calls a Markov blanket: everything between inside and outside passes through it.

What it expected (prior): p(x) = N(m₀, s₀²), m₀ = 4, s₀² = 4 What it feels: o = 10 − x + noise. The pin is drawn at y = 10 − o, the distance the warmth suggests. It assumes noise of variance σ² = 1/τ. τ is the precision: the Trust slider, 0.01 to 7.2, and 1 unless you move it. The real noise has standard deviation 0.5, redrawn every 8 pictures. Its guess (belief): q(x) = N(μ, s²)

F = E_q[ ln q(x) − ln p(o, x) ] = ½[ ln(s₀²/s²) + (s² + (μ − m₀)²)/s₀² − 1 ] complexity + ½[ ln(2πσ²) + ((y − μ)² + s²)/σ² ] minus accuracy = surprise + gap surprise = −ln p(o) = ½ ln(2π(s₀² + σ²)) + (y − m₀)² / (2(s₀² + σ²)) gap = KL[ q || the exact answer ] ≥ 0, so F ≥ surprise, always.

The two springs are the two squared terms: the blue one has stiffness 1/s₀² and pulls μ to m₀, the copper one has stiffness τ and pulls μ to y. Their stored stretch is exactly those terms of F. The rest of F depends only on the widths of the hills, which is why the pile never reaches zero.

Thinking: dμ/dt = −0.4 ∂F/∂μ = −0.4 [ (μ − m₀)/s₀² + τ(μ − y) ] d ln s²/dt = −0.8 ∂F/∂ln s² = −0.4 [ s²(1/s₀² + τ) − 1 ] Moving: the creature stands at c on the rail, so x = lamp − c, and dc/dt = −0.6 ∂F/∂c = 0.6 τ(y − μ)

Time is counted in pictures, twelve a second. Scene 3 is filmed at quarter speed so the gap can be seen closing. Both rules are plain gradient descent on the one quantity F. Left alone, thinking arrives at the textbook answer: μ = (m₀/s₀² + τy)/(1/s₀² + τ), s² = 1/(1/s₀² + τ). With trust near zero that is the prior; with very high trust it is the pin.

In scene 2 each button restarts from the same cold moment: lamp at 7.5 hands, the creature having already worked out that it is cold (μ = 6.8). "Move only" then makes the feeling match that stale guess and stops. Had its guess been frozen exactly at 4, moving alone would have brought it home in this small model; what goes wrong is acting on a guess that is never checked.

The two doors

Behind a door the lamp is at distance d. Belief: d ~ N(m, v). Known room: m = 6, v = 0.05. Dark room: m = 6.5, v = 4 before any glimpse. A glimpse is a reading with noise variance 2: v after n glimpses = 1/(1/4 + n/2). Inside a room it feels with σ² = 0.25, so it expects to feel N(m, V), V = v + 0.25. It likes to feel what it always expected: N(4, v*), v* = 4 + 0.25. risk = KL[ N(m, V) || N(4, v*) ] = ½[ ln(v*/V) + (V + (m − 4)²)/v* − 1 ] ambiguity = ½ ln(2πe σ²) = 0.73 for both doors G = risk + ambiguity (expected free energy; the lower pile wins) The same G, cut the other way: G = expected miss − expected learning expected miss = ½ ln(2πv*) + (V + (m − 4)²)/(2v*) expected learning = ½ ln(V/σ²) (the empty crate on top of each pile)

The creature takes the door with the lower G. With these numbers the choice flips between the sixth and seventh glimpse (the formula gives 6.4). The slider assumes each glimpse shows what was expected, so only the width of the belief changes, not its middle. Ambiguity is the same for both doors because its skin is equally noisy in both rooms; in a room where the feeling said little about the place, that block would be taller and the creature would keep away.

What this leaves out

Nearly everything about real creatures. One hidden number, a straight-line lamp (real warmth falls with the square of distance), bell-shaped beliefs that happen to be exactly right for this problem, no learning of the model itself (the third way to lower free energy: here m₀, s₀² and the rule linking warmth to distance never change), no body to keep alive, plans only one step deep, and a creature that always takes the lower pile rather than choosing with some randomness.

What is disputed

The free energy principle is a principle and a way of building models, not a finding that one experiment could refute; Karl Friston, its author, says so himself. Treating what a creature wants as what it expects (scene 2) is a choice the theory makes, and critics ask whether it explains wanting or only renames it. How far the Markov blankets drawn in models match real boundaries in the world is argued over, and whether brains actually do sums like these is an open question. The name free energy is borrowed from physics, where the same sum appears; the creature cannot work out its surprise directly, but it can always push free energy down, and free energy is a lid on surprise.
