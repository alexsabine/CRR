    What this is, and is not

    One lump of clay, one wheel, one pair of hands. It is a way of seeing how one person shapes another without breaking them: by holding still, by pressing just enough, by going at the pace the other can bear, and by letting go.

    The model

    The pot is a stack of rings. Clay is never made or lost: whatever a touch frees, the rim rises to use. A touch is a meeting of two firmnesses. Each turn the clay moves a share of the way to the hand, and that share is hand ÷ (hand + clay). Thick, drier clay holds its shape; a thin wet wall gives way to almost anything. This is the same rule a Kalman filter uses to weigh what it already believes against what arrives, and the same weighting the free energy principle calls precision.

    A still hand centres the clay, because once a turn the high side meets it. A moving hand does not. Speed throws anything off-centre further out and opens thin walls. Every change is counted; changes made and then unmade are the surplus, and they tire the clay.

    What it leaves out

    What the clay “wants” is put in by this model. Each lump is given a form it yields to most easily, so that the meeting has two sides. Real clay wants nothing. And clay is compliant in a way no child is: it cannot refuse, imagine, or change the potter. The piece is about how to use weight with someone who can do all three, and the aim is that they come through as themselves.

    The balance in the film is the potter’s weight divided by the clay’s firmness. At one, they are matched. One finding surprised me and is worth knowing: the potter who forces the clay does not read as heavy on the balance, because clay that is being forced resists harder. The harm shows as strain in the wall, not on the balance.

    The voice is synthetic, and the same one as the museum films: Piper, model en_GB-vctk-medium, speaker p225, slowed a little, with light compression and room. It was trained on the CSTR VCTK Corpus (University of Edinburgh), CC BY 4.0. The corpus is recordings of real volunteers, so the voice is derived from a real person’s, though it is not presented as theirs.

    Real clay is far richer than this: it shears, it has grain and memory, it dries unevenly, and a potter uses two hands, a sponge and a rib at once. The numbers are the model’s own and are not measurements of clay or of people. The link to people is an analogy offered for teaching, not a finding.

    Where the ideas come from

    Holding, the good-enough carer and graduated failure: D. W. Winnicott (1953, 1960). Potential space, play, and the question never to be asked: Winnicott, Playing and Reality (1971). The hundred languages of children, and the image of the child as rich and capable: L. Malaguzzi, tr. L. Gandini. Against the learner as an empty vessel: P. Freire (1970). Centring as a way of life: M. C. Richards (1964). The fixed point: L. E. J. Brouwer (1911). The partial view: D. Haraway, Situated Knowledges (1988). The reading of the Now as an empty place between the settled past and the open future is an interpretation. Rousseau’s tutor in Emile (1762) arranges the child’s world with care; the “noble savage” is not his phrase. Blake’s quarrel with Rousseau is in Jerusalem, plate 52, “To the Deists”, where he also writes that a person “must continually be changed into his direct Contrary”. Equanimity ordinarily means evenness of mind; the sense used here, the past carried at its earned weight, is this film’s own.
