The Tyger’s forge

About this piece

What is computed. The fire is a heat balance on a lump of burning charcoal: carbon burns as fast as the bellows bring oxygen, and heat leaves by radiation, with the hot gases, and through the hearth. The colour of everything that glows comes from Planck’s law for a black body, weighed by the eye’s colour matching functions (CIE 1931). Heat runs along the iron by the heat equation, solved as you watch in a hundred cells, and leaves each cell by radiation and convection. A blow carries momentum and energy; hot iron is soft, so the energy does plastic work and the iron spreads, and each cell keeps its volume exactly. Thin places cool faster than thick ones, which is why the bar goes striped as it fades. Sparks are flakes of scale on ballistic paths, cooling by radiation, coloured by their own temperature. The anvil’s ring is a sum of decaying modes, excited by the impulse and the length of the contact, made in the browser. In the trough the iron first wears a jacket of steam and cools slowly (the Leidenfrost effect); when the jacket fails the water boils hard. The numbers on the pyrometer are the model’s own.

What this leaves out, and what is squeezed. No screen can show iron at 1300 °C some ten thousand times brighter than iron at 700 °C. The hue is the computed one; the brightness is the true luminance raised to the power 0.55, and very bright colours wash towards white as they do in an eye or a camera. Real black-body light at 1300 °C is still orange on paper: it looks near white because it is so bright. The fire is one temperature, not a bed with a hot heart and a cool crust. The iron conducts along its length only. Heat lost into the anvil, drag on the sparks, and the chemistry of burning iron are left out or stood in for by simple rules. The anvil’s notes were chosen by ear, not measured. Flames, steam and the flicker of the light are drawn, though how much of each there is comes from the model. The exposure opens in a dim room and closes in the blast, as an eye does, so the swatches on the pyrometer (fixed exposure) can look dimmer than the picture when the fire is slack. Light thrown back by the floor and the brick is stood in for by two small fill lights, and a little night sky comes in through the open wall. Constants marked “chosen” in the notes are not measurements.

What is art. The stripes in the forest, the eyes, and the rain of starlight are not physics. Each tree lights in step with one cut in the iron, and its twin lights on the other side: that mirror is the piece’s reading of “fearful symmetry”, not Blake’s. Their colour and strength follow the fire. No tiger is drawn.

Blake’s words. “The Tyger”, from Songs of Experience (1794), complete, in the reading of D. V. Erdman’s edition, which keeps Blake’s own spelling (“sieze”) and pointing. Public domain. Nothing else on this page is quoted.
