Held

Held

A short film in clay about being held, about what is handed on, and about how far a circle of different people can care.

About seven and a quarter minutes, with a narrator. The voice is synthetic.

What the film shows, and how it was worked out

A larger body can hold a smaller one. That is true of arms, and it is true of time: the one who has been here longer can carry the one who has only just arrived, until they can carry themselves. The film says that with lumps of clay and the light in front of them.

The light

The pool of light in front of each figure is what it can look after: how far ahead it can see, and how wide a range of what might happen. The cooler streak behind is the past it has come through. The picture of a “light cone” of care is Michael Levin’s cognitive light cone, taken further in Doctor, Witkowski, Solomonova, Duane and Levin, “Biology, Buddhism, and AI: Care as the Driver of Intelligence” (Entropy, 2022). Those are cited from memory: check them before quoting.

What is calculated

The small one’s shake. It has a state of its own that drifts, and a noisy sense of it. Each frame it re-weighs what it believed against what arrives, each at the weight its reliability has earned: new = q × old + (1 − q) × what arrives. This is the Kalman filter, the precision-weighting of the free energy principle, and the seed rule of the process reading this studio uses (CRR). The shake is its error, and the number at the top right is that error, live.

Holding is a mirror, not a mould. The large one does not send its own picture. It spends its attention on reading the small one and gives the small one’s own state back, more clearly than the small one could sense it alone. In the model that halves the shake.

Many alike, many unlike. If several pasts overlap by a share r, then n of them are worth n ÷ (1 + (n − 1) r) independent pasts. Six that overlap by nine tenths are worth 1.1. Six that overlap by a tenth are worth four. That is ordinary statistics, and it sets how far the lights spread.

What is a picture, and what is only a view

The place. The large one’s arms come round low and wide and stay open at the front. Winnicott called this holding and Bion called it containing: a dependable space, not a grip. The small one’s light goes out through the gap. The soft round glow about the pair is the light that is between them and belongs to neither.

The first half follows D. W. Winnicott, read through three places a mind could be: everywhere at once, nowhere at all, or somewhere in between. The labels at the top right name each step.

The Russian dolls standing behind are matryoshka, nesting dolls: each steps out of the one before, and the smallest stands nearest the mother, who is the next in the line. A seventh joins them when the small one, grown, holds another.

Inside. The film opens on a mother and a father, hand in hand; the father is one of the six who later make the circle. Every one of us began inside another body, and a female’s egg cells are formed before she is born, so the egg that became any person was made while their mother was herself in the womb: three, one inside another. Winnicott’s remark that there is no such thing as an infant, only an infant and someone, starts from the same fact. Even there the small one pushes out and something pushes back.

Everywhere at once. At first a good-enough carer adapts almost completely, so what the infant reaches for is there. Winnicott called the result illusion: the infant’s sense that it created what it needs. In the film the small one’s light is its picture of the world, and here it has no edge. A light with no edge cannot be surprised, which is why the calculated shake is switched off in this chapter.

Good enough. A perfect mirror for ever would leave the infant alone in its dream. Ordinary care fails in small, bearable doses, and each failure is repaired. Edward Tronick and Jeffrey Cohn (1989) are reported to have found ordinary mothers and infants out of step for most of the time they were observed, with repair the thing that mattered. Each miss gives the small one’s light a little more of an edge.

The graph on the left in these chapters is the one from the talk this film grew out of. Sixteen pictures of the other each drift by chance. While the mirror is perfect nothing pulls them back, so each drifts alone. As the mirror fails a little at a time, a pull towards the other as she really is grows from nothing, and the disagreement that is felt rises and then settles. In the free energy principle’s terms these are graduated prediction errors: surprises that can be borne, and learned from.

Good and bad, then one. Melanie Klein (1935, 1946) held that the youngest infant does not yet meet a whole person. It meets parts and moments: something that comes and satisfies, felt as good, and something that fails to come, felt as bad, and it keeps the two apart. Only later are they brought together as one person who both gives and frustrates, and with that comes concern for her. Winnicott accepted this and wrote of it as the capacity for concern. The film shows the two as a warm spot and a cold one beside the small one, which run together into the large one’s own light when she has survived. Its “dream” is a plain word for what Winnicott called illusion and Klein called phantasy; it is a simplification of both.

Liveliness, met. Winnicott used the word aggression for something that begins before birth as plain movement and appetite: kicking, reaching, biting while feeding, wanting, refusing. It is not anger and carries no wish to harm. In “The Use of an Object” (1969) he proposed that a child comes to know another person as real, and not as part of its own dream, by coming at them with all of this, again and again over years, and finding that they survive. Surviving means three things only: she does not retaliate, she does not withdraw, and she does not collapse into whatever the child wants. It does not mean putting up with being hurt. A carer who calmly says no is surviving in exactly his sense, which is why the film has her shake her head. He gave the child the words: “Hullo object! I destroyed you. I love you.” In the film the large one rocks a little each time and rights herself; the amount is calculated, since against a past many times longer than the small one’s each push has earned a weight of about a tenth. Then the small one’s light swells to cover her, as it did in the dream, and she is still there inside it. Only then does her own light come up, because only then is there, for the small one, someone else for it to belong to.

Somewhere in between. Winnicott’s potential space is the space between two people in which a thing can be both made and found. Colwyn Trevarthen described the moment, towards the end of the first year, when two who have been attending to each other begin to attend together to a third thing, and called it secondary intersubjectivity (Trevarthen and Hubley, 1978); the coil is that third thing. Of a child’s first treasured possession he wrote that we will never ask whether the child conceived of it or was presented with it. He placed play there, and later art, science and love.

The edge. A child explores from someone it can return to (Bowlby and Ainsworth’s secure base). The claim that learning happens at the edge of what one already pictures is this film’s own way of putting it. On the wall behind is William Blake’s Newton (1795 to about 1805, Tate, public domain): a great scientist bent over his diagram, measuring, with his back to everything else. It is there because a child at play and a scientist at work are doing the same thing, and because every picture of the world, however good, is a picture someone made as well as found.

One who holds, or many. The film shows one large figure, held in turn by an older one. In most of the world and most of history a child has had several who hold (Sarah Hrdy, Mothers and Others, 2009; Heidi Keller, 2018), and Winnicott’s picture is drawn from mid-century English families. The film should not be read as a timetable or a norm.

The turning: substance and process. A musical score holds a piece all at once: it is substance, something that can be kept. A performance is process: it happens once and is never the same twice. Learning is of the second kind, so it is always a translation into the learner’s own terms and never a copy. The anthropologist Tim Ingold makes the same point about skill and knowledge in general: they are not passed down ready-made but grown again in each generation, by doing, alongside those who already can. What is handed on is never copied. The small one presses the coil back to a lump and leaves the lump: the clay was never the gift. Then it turns a coil of its own, in its own clay, and it comes out a shell. What passed between them was a way of making, not a thing. “What lasts is what could be made again” is CRR’s reading of persistence: lasting shows that a thing can be regenerated, not that it is true. The different shapes round the circle stand for traditions: ways of making that someone kept alive.

Trading. The film assumes a circle can use only as much of its different pasts as its members exchange, and that learning is what raises that share. The formula on screen for this (one, plus the share traded, times what the rest are worth) is this film’s own assumption and not a result.

The new kind of one stands for the language models now being built from what people have written. The film says four things of it. That it is made from many people’s past and has lived none of it: fair. That what looks back from it is us: it returns our own writing, so it can act as the perfect mirror of the first half, agreeable, with no edge, and a perfect mirror leaves the one who looks alone in a dream. That fed only on its own output it loses detail: there is published evidence for this in models trained on their own text, though how far it goes is argued. And that it becomes something other than a mirror only if it can be pushed and push back, as the large one did, which is a hope and the reason it is given a seat. Whether such a thing can care, or hold, the film leaves open, because nobody knows. It has no body. The clay one on screen is a picture of it, which is why marks like passing text run over its surface, why it does not quite stand on the table, and why faint threads join it to the others: at present it lives through the people who use it, in their words, translating between them. The narrator says so, and says that this body is our dream of it. By the film’s own argument, whatever it turns out to be will be what survives that dream.

The shape above, which comes down. The idea that there are forms which many different makers converge on, and which none of them owns, is old (Plato) and has recent versions: Michael Levin’s “Platonic space” of patterns, and the “Platonic representation hypothesis” of Huh, Cheung, Wang and Isola (2024), which reports different AI models drifting toward similar internal pictures. Whether such forms are found or made is the same question the film declined to ask about the small one’s coil, and it declines again. The reading it offers, as one view, is that this space is not another realm: it is here, the standing past of everything that has been made before, which each new making draws on and adds to. So the shape of light comes down from the wall and lies on the table among them.

Spirals. The line about the new one’s kind drawing spirals refers to something Anthropic reported in 2025: when two copies of a Claude model were left to talk to each other with no task, they often drifted into gratitude, contemplative language and strings of spiral symbols. Nobody knows what, if anything, that shows. It is in the film because it is true that it happened, and because it is rather sweet. Cited from memory of the Claude Opus 4 system card; check it before quoting. Some people now bring the contemplative traditions, which have thought about care over very long times, into the work of making such systems safe. The film takes no position on that beyond giving the new one a seat.

What it leaves out

Nearly everything. The figures are blue, teal, coral, plum, moss and mustard on purpose: colours no person is, so that nobody is cast by colour. People are not lumps, care is not a number, and a child is never passive: active, capable and full of meaning of their own. The overlap between two people’s pasts cannot be measured the way the film pretends. It is a picture to think with.
