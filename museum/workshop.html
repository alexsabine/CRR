The copper

About this piece

What is computed. The colour is a small real model. The sheet is ruled into squares 1.4 mm across. Each dab leaves colour under the dabber, thickest at its centre; a film of 1 is a good one. As the bed goes under the roller, strip by strip, every square gives the paper a share of what lies on it: 30% at the lightest pressure, 75% at the heaviest. Where the film is thicker than the roller allows (2 at the lightest, 1 at the heaviest), four fifths of the excess is pushed ahead as a wave, and past the edge of the plate it blots the paper. On the relief strip, colour down in the hollows prints only where it lies deeper than 0.5 (light) to 0.2 (heavy). Nothing is made or lost: what was dabbed on equals what stays on the copper plus what is on the paper. Plate and paper are the same squares, mirrored.

What this leaves out. Colour drying on the plate, wet colours running together (they are averaged), damp paper stretching or slipping, and the mottle of the pull, which is drawn, not computed. The shares and limits are chosen to behave sensibly; they are not measurements. Blake's ink for relief plates was oil-based, and here the strip takes the same colours as the plate. The strip, its word and the drying line are ours: Blake is not known to have printed a word plate with this design. Your own pull is finished in pen only.

More to try. Three further small models, each stated in the notes. The bite: depth grows at a rate set by the strength of the acid and by how much gas shields the copper; the acid is used up; the etch eats sideways as far as it eats down. The wash: water and pigment on the same squares; water runs freely between wet squares and only creeps into dry ones, carries pigment with it, and dries fastest at an edge, which is why a wash dries with a dark rim; pigment is neither made nor lost. The night: one candle and a globe of water that obeys the lens rule, 1/s + 1/s' = 1/f. Their rates are chosen, not measured.

What is not settled. Essick dated the plate about 1793; Viscomi argues for 1795. For the colour-printed pages of the books, Essick and Viscomi argue for one pull with ink and colour together, Phillips for two pulls in register. The etched lines are a reconstruction traced from the finished print.

Sources. J. Viscomi, Blake and the Idea of the Book (1993) and "Blake's Annus Mirabilis: The Productions of 1795" (2007); R. N. Essick, William Blake, Printmaker (1980); R. N. Essick and J. Viscomi, "An Inquiry into William Blake's Method of Color Printing" (2002); M. Phillips, William Blake: The Creation of the Songs (2000). The door to Felpham

This room is Lambeth, about 1795. In 1800 the Blakes took the press sixty miles south to a cottage by the sea at Felpham, and stayed three years. Through the door it is an evening there, with a table laid in the garden.
