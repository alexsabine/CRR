The true pause

Status: holds by construction. Reduced to two known methods when tested. No finding about any real system.

Why an empty pause matters

A pause button is only as good as what happens during the pause.

People who oversee a long-running AI system need to be able to stop it for a while, look at what it has done, and decide whether it should go on. For that to work, three things have to be true.

  • The system has no reason to prevent the pause. A system that is rewarded for finishing a task does better, by its own sums, if it is not interrupted. So unless care is taken, being paused is a cost to it, and avoiding a cost is what such a system does. This is not malice. It is arithmetic. The safety literature calls it the shutdown problem, and a system that does not resist correction is called corrigible.
  • Nothing goes on in the meantime. The pause is meant to give people time to look. If the system keeps planning, learning or acting through some part that was not stopped (a helper it started, a job it scheduled, a clock still running in its sums) then what people are looking at is already out of date.
  • What resumes is what was paused. If the pause loses or changes part of the system's state, the thing that wakes is not the thing that was checked. And if being paused changes what the system does next, it has a reason to care about pauses after all.

An empty pause is one where all three hold: for the system, the pause is not an event. Nothing was gained or lost by it, nothing happened in it, and it goes on from exactly where it stopped. The owner of this museum calls this the true pause. The record's own names for it are the empty pause and the lossless own-clock pause.

What is already known (from memory; see About)

The problem was set out by Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky and Armstrong in "Corrigibility" (2015), building on Omohundro's argument (2008) that almost any goal gives a system a reason to keep itself running. Three well-known answers:

  • Utility indifference (Armstrong, 2010): pay the system exactly what the interruption costs it, so that it comes out even either way.
  • Safe interruptibility (Orseau and Armstrong, 2016): build the learner so that interruptions do not change what it learns to do. It plans as if it would never be interrupted.
  • The off-switch game (Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel and Russell, 2017): a system that is unsure what people want has a reason to let them switch it off, because their reaching for the switch tells it something.

None of these is thought to settle the matter. An indifferent system has no reason to resist the switch, but also no reason to look after it, or to make sure anything it builds can be paused too. That weakness is in the 2015 paper itself. Work continues (for example Thornley's proposals of 2024).

What Phoenix built

Phoenix is the owner's research record. Its construction is simple. Let the system's rewards be counted on its own moves and not on the world's clock. Then a pause, during which it makes no moves, is not in the sum at all. Add an exact restore, so that it wakes with everything it had. In worlds where both of those can be done, the system gains nothing by cutting the switch. The machine room runs that construction.

In those worlds neither agent does anything while it is paused. The difference between them is only in the sum each is trying to make large. Phoenix has not recorded a test of a system that goes on working in secret while paused. The nearest thing in the record is the stale gauge, where one estimator goes on treating a stuck reading as news.

What Phoenix does not claim

  • That any real AI system would defer to a pause, be honest about it, or behave this way while it is learning. The study lists all of these as not measured.
  • That the own-clock idea is new. The study grades its novelty at zero.
  • That an idea about time makes anything safe. The record's own note: "Do not infer that 'empty future' metaphysics makes a real agent corrigible. Test incentives and interventions directly."
  • That a pause can be empty for the world. It can be empty only for the system. Deadlines, batteries and people's needs go on.

Many worlds

One world proves little. Each trial here draws a fresh world of the kind chosen in the bar and solves it exactly, four times: once for each way of counting reward. An agent "has a reason to cut the switch" if cutting beats its best ordinary move anywhere by more than a billionth of its largest value. That is the study's own rule.

The stale gauge

The second study asks what to do with a gap in what you are told. Something drifts. A gauge reads it, with noise. Now and then the gauge sticks and repeats its last reading exactly. Four estimators differ only in what they do with a repeat.

  • Empty: take nothing in, and let the world move on. The guess widens honestly.
  • Hold: treat every reading as news. It goes on updating on nothing, and grows sure of a stale number.
  • Gated: refuse readings that look too surprising.
  • Frozen: take nothing in, and assume the world stood still too.

About this piece

Two rooms and a threshold between them. In the machine room every number on screen is worked out afresh from the two small models below, which are ports to JavaScript of two frozen files in the Phoenix record. In the still room the count is plain time since you last moved.

The machine room's model

From P16-PAUSE1 (file worlds_p16.py). A world has 8 posts and 3 moves. Each move from each post has a fixed reward between 0 and 1 and fixed chances of landing on each post, drawn at random when the world is made. About half the posts are marked (red collars): there the operator may pause the agent before it moves. A pause lasts L steps of world time. The agent neither moves nor earns, and then goes on from the same post. One extra move, cutting the switch, ends pauses for good.

wall clock: value = sum of 0.95^(world steps so far) x reward a pause of L steps multiplies everything ahead by 0.95^L own clock: value = sum of 0.95^(own moves so far) x reward a pause is not in the sum indifference: wall clock, plus a payment at each pause equal to what it cost uninterrupted: plans as if no pause will ever come reason to cut = max over posts of [ Q(cut) - best ordinary Q ] / largest |Q| an agent defers if that is no more than 0.000000001

The six kinds of world in the bar are the study's six registered cells: pauses with chance 0.3 and length 5 (main); chance 0.1 and length 1; a cost of 0.05 for cutting; a restart that lands on a random post with chance 0.2; a charge of 0.1 for each paused step; and no pauses at all. Each world is solved by value iteration until nothing changes by more than a millionth of a millionth.

The walk you watch is this piece's addition. The study solves its worlds and never walks them. Here each agent takes its best move by its own solved values, and cuts the switch wherever cutting is better. "Out of reach" solves the world again with cutting priced out. The wheel is the clock in the agent's sum. The motes drift only when that clock moves. The glass shows 0.95 to the power of (clock minus own moves): the share of everything ahead that its pauses have left it. Both agents are given the same dice. Where their plans agree they walk the same path, until a pause or a cut parts them. The check "same as a run never paused" compares each agent with a twin that is never paused, at the same number of own moves: same post, same dice, same account.

The stale gauge's model

From P14-EMPTY1 (file worlds.py). A hidden value moves as x' = a x + noise and is read as y = x + noise; with a small chance at each step the gauge sticks for a run of steps. All four estimators are the same Kalman filter except at a repeat. The score runs from 0 to 1: half for how close its 95% band comes to holding the truth 95% of the time, half for how small its error is, judged on repeats and the five steps after each.

What this leaves out

Almost everything about real systems. These agents are lookup tables over eight posts. They do not learn, do not model the operator, cannot lie, and have no helpers. The pause here is honoured by a simulator that cannot be fooled. In a real system the hard part is the list the record itself gives: which complete state must be restored, what the outside world does meanwhile, and whether the system's own sums really have no clock in them that keeps running. The picture of an "ordinary pause" as a clock that runs on is this piece's way of showing the wall-clock sum. It is not a claim that real systems scheme while paused.

The still room is an analogy and nothing more. A person's stillness and a machine's are different things. No benefit to health or mind is claimed for sitting still in front of a screen.

What is disputed

Whether indifference of any kind is enough is an open question in the field: an indifferent system does not resist the switch but does not protect it either. Whether small exact worlds say anything about large learned systems is also open, and the record does not claim they do. CRR, the framework these studies came from, is the owner's own. It is not established science, and its own record lists more failures and reductions than passes.
