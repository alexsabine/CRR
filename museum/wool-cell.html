The smallest cell, in wool

Goodsell’s painting is the pattern sheet. In front of it the same cell, crocheted, cut open and kept growing. Drag to look round; click to pull focus.

Pattern: cell membrane

Worked in a spiral, do not join. Make two cups, pole to middle; they meet at the waist.

    Painting: “JCVI-syn3A Minimal Cell” (2022). Illustration by David S. Goodsell, RCSB Protein Data Bank. doi: 10.2210/rcsb_pdb/goodsell-gallery-042. CC BY 4.0, reduced.

    About this piece

    JCVI-syn3A is a bacterium whose genome was written by people and cut down until little could be removed without killing it: one circular chromosome of 543 thousand base pairs carrying 493 genes (M. Breuer and colleagues, eLife 8, 2019). It is very nearly the smallest genome known to run a free-living cell (its parent, syn3.0, is a little smaller but divides badly), and about a fifth of its genes still have no known job.

    The model

    The membrane is counted in stitches: one stitch stands for one patch of lipid. A newborn cell is the ordinary crochet ball of 300 stitches, read as two cups of 150 worked from each pole to the middle. The contents (volume) grow by the same fraction every minute and double in 120 model minutes. Lipid is made in step with that growth: stitches added = the lipid setting × 300 × the rise in volume. New stitches go on at the waist: three plain rounds on each cup, then the four decrease rounds that close a ball (24, 18, 12, 6). The bag holds only what its shape allows (two spherical caps, worked out from the rounds done) and stretches by 6 % at most; if the contents would not fit, growth stops. The chromosome is copied at a steady rate and is finished after 90 model minutes.

    The cell divides when the closing rounds are done (600 stitches: the surface has doubled), the volume is twice what it was at birth, and the copy is complete. Stitches made after the closing rounds gather in a bleb, which buds off as a small ball of 36, 48 or 72 stitches (picked by a seeded random number), taking its share of the contents. Everything on the row counter and the pattern card is this model’s own state.

    The two things you can change

    Lipid. At 1.0 surface and volume double together and the cell divides every 120 minutes. At 0.8 or below the bag is too small for what is in it: it goes tight, growth stalls and it never divides. At 0.9 it manages two divisions, a little tighter each time, and stalls in the third generation. Above 1.0 the closing rounds finish before there is enough inside for two cells: the cell goes baggy and buds off blobs that have no chromosome.

    Chromosome. With “Copy finishes first” off, the copying runs at 0.6 of its speed and the pinch does not wait for it. One daughter keeps the chromosome; the other gets none and does not go on. The model then unpicks the unfinished copy and starts it again.

    What is real, and how sure

    Checked against the papers’ own summaries: JCVI-syn3.0 had 531 thousand base pairs and 473 genes, 149 of them of unknown function at the time (C. Hutchison and colleagues, Science 351, 2016). Its cells grew into irregular shapes; putting back 19 genes gave syn3A, which divides normally, and seven of those, among them ftsZ and sepF, are needed together (J. Pelletier and colleagues, Cell 184, 2021). A whole-cell model of syn3A follows its chemistry through a cell cycle (Z. Thornburg and colleagues, Cell 185, 2022).

    Quoted from memory and not rechecked for this page: the real doubling time of about two hours (reports run from about 105 to 120 minutes); about 500 ribosomes in a cell about 400 nanometres across, from electron tomograms, so each pompom pair here stands for about fifty; and “about a fifth” of genes without a known job (roughly 90 of 452 protein-coding genes in the 2019 paper). One small mismatch in the sources: 473 + 19 is 492, while the syn3A papers say 493.

    What this leaves out

    Nearly everything. There is no metabolism, no reading of genes and no chance: a real cell’s timing wanders, this one’s does not. Real lipid goes in all over the membrane, not at the waist, and a real membrane’s shape depends on bending and tension that are not modelled. Nobody has shown that lipid supply sets when syn3A divides, or that it checks its chromosome before pinching: those are this model’s rules. The irregular shapes of syn3.0 came from missing genes; whether too much membrane was the cause is not known, so the likeness here is one of shape. The surface-to-volume figure assumes a newborn sphere 400 nanometres across. We follow one daughter; her sister is set aside on the bench, though in life she would carry on.
