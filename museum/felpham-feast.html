Felpham, Sussex · late September 1801 · the garden

A visionary feast at Felpham

About this piece

What is computed. The Sun's place in the sky, from the latitude of Felpham and the date. The colour of the sky and of the sunlight, by following light through an atmosphere of air, haze and ozone (single scattering). The sea: four trains of waves that obey the dispersion rule for water of a given depth, slow and steepen as the beach shoals, turn towards the shore, and break when they are about four fifths as high as the water is deep; the swash then runs up the sand by Hunt's rule and falls back under gravity. Candles: each burns down at a stated rate, leans and dims in the breeze, and blows out in a hard gust. The cloth is a net of some 1,800 points joined by threads that keep their length. The glasses bend what is behind them by Snell's law, and the bright curves they throw on the cloth are traced ray by ray. Hot dishes cool by Newton's law and stop steaming. Drink poured from the jug is counted: nothing is made or lost. The numbers shown are the model's own.

What this leaves out. The Moon, the tide (which is large here), real star places, light scattered more than once (a rough term stands in for it), shadows cast by the Sun, wind on the waves. The glow inside wax and fruit is an approximation, not a transport calculation. The mist round the candles is thicker than real air so that the halo shows. The night sky is brighter than a moonless night so that you can see the shore. Constants marked "chosen" in the notes are not measurements.

What is not history. No record says what the Blakes ate on any evening. The food is what a cottage on this coast could have put out in a September after two dear years: it is a guess, named plainly. The cottage still stands, a few minutes' walk from the sea; here the walk is shortened. The guests are his visions, shown as figures of light and as no one in particular. The fiddle tune is made up by this page.

Blake's words. Letters to John Flaxman (21 September 1800) and to Thomas Butts (2 October 1800 and 22 November 1802); "Auguries of Innocence" (about 1803); "A Vision of the Last Judgment" (notebook, 1810). The fairy funeral is not in his hand: Allan Cunningham printed it in 1830 as something Blake said. All are in the public domain.
