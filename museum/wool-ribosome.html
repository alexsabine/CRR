The ribosome, in wool

A string of beads is read three at a time. Each three call for one bobble. The right carrier has to turn up by chance; the wrong ones are tried and sent back. Drag to look around. Click anything to pull focus.

About this bench

The beads are a messenger RNA: four colours for A, U, G and C, three beads to a codon. The two purple lumps are the small and large halves of a ribosome. Each L-shaped piece in the basket is a transfer RNA carrying one amino acid as a bobble. When the right one docks, the hook stitches its bobble onto the chain, the string moves on by three beads, and the chain is pushed out through the tunnel.

The model

Reading starts at the first AUG and goes in threes by the standard genetic code: all 64 codons, with UAA, UAG and UGA as stops (NCBI translation table 1; worked out by Nirenberg, Khorana and others, 1961 to 1966).

Twelve pictures are taken each second. In each picture where the docking place (the A site) is free, a carrier arrives with probability 0.7, drawn evenly from the 21 kinds in the basket: twenty transfer RNAs, one for each amino acid, and the release factor that answers a stop. It is tried for one picture. Only the matching kind is kept, so on average twenty are sent back for each one kept. Stitching and moving on take six pictures. That comes to about one amino acid every five seconds. The draws come from a seeded generator (seed 1): the same seed and the same message always give the same run.

How fast is the real thing?

A ribosome in a growing bacterium adds about 20 amino acids a second (Bremer and Dennis, EcoSal Plus, 2008, give roughly 12 to 22 for E. coli, faster in richer broth). This bench runs about a hundred times slower so you can see it. A real ribosome also meets many wrong carriers for each right one.

The messages

Oxytocin: Cys-Tyr-Ile-Gln-Asn-Cys-Pro-Leu-Gly, the nine amino acids of the hormone (sequence: du Vigneaud and colleagues, 1953). Met-enkephalin: Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Met, one of the body's own opioids (Hughes and colleagues, Nature, 1975). In the body both are cut out of longer chains. The AUG at the front and the stop at the end are ours, and each codon is one that spells the right amino acid, not necessarily the one the human gene uses.

Choose a bead and change it: the meaning may stay the same (a silent change), one bobble may change, or the chain may stop early. Delete a bead and every codon after it is read out of step.

Tetracycline

Tetracycline sits in the small half where an arriving transfer RNA would dock, so nothing more can be added (Brodersen and colleagues, Cell, 2000). A bacterium that cannot make protein cannot grow; our own ribosomes are built differently, and our cells take up little of the drug, so we are spared.

What this leaves out

The helper proteins that start, drive and end the work (initiation, elongation and release factors beyond the one shown) and the GTP they spend. The fifty-odd proteins and three RNAs a real ribosome is made of. The enzymes that load each transfer RNA with its amino acid, which is where the code is really enforced. The several transfer RNAs behind most amino acids, their unequal numbers, and the loose pairing at the third bead. Near misses and the two-step proofreading that rejects them, and the rare mistake that gets through (somewhere between one in a thousand and one in ten thousand). The thirty or so amino acids a real tunnel hides (ours hides four). Folding. The drifting in the basket is set dressing: the draw is the model.

Why it is in this lab

The ribosome is ancient, mostly RNA, and shared by all life. The part that joins the amino acids is RNA, not protein (Nissen and colleagues, Science, 2000). That is one of the strongest clues we have about the last common ancestor. How the first ribosome came to be is not settled.
