About this piece

This is ATP synthase, the machine that makes most of the ATP in most living things, made in wool. The ring in the membrane is the rotor (the c-ring). The blue lump beside it is the stator, with a way in at the top and a way out at the bottom. The bent axle hangs from the ring into a head of six lobes, three pale and three orange, which the long blue arm stops from turning. The orange lobes do the work: as the bend of the axle comes round, each in turn is open, then loose, then tight.

What you are watching

A proton (red) steps onto a segment at the top of the stator, the ring clicks round one segment, and the proton that has ridden all the way round steps off underneath. With a ring of N segments, N protons make one turn, and one turn makes three ATP. So an ATP costs N/3 protons: 2.7 with the cow’s ring of 8, 3.3 with the ring of 10 in yeast and E. coli, 4.7 with the ring of 14 in spinach chloroplasts. Rings from 8 to 15 are known in ATP synthases, and a close relative that only pumps has 17. The yellow beads are phosphates: ADP is two, ATP is three, and the tight lobe presses a loose one on. One segment is darker than the rest so that you can count the turns.

The model

The push on each proton is the proton-motive force, Δp = Δψ − (2.303 RT/F) ΔpH, in millivolts. Δψ is the voltage, top side minus bottom side; ΔpH is the pH above minus the pH below. 2.303 RT/F is 61.5 mV at 37 °C and 59.2 mV at 25 °C (R = 8.314 J/mol/K, F = 96,485 C/mol). N/3 protons falling through Δp give (N/3) F Δp of energy per ATP. Making an ATP in a working cell costs about 50 kJ/mol, and 50 kJ/mol is the value used here (in a test tube at standard concentrations it is nearer 30; cells keep ATP high and ADP low, which raises the price). The turbine stalls where the two are equal: at 194 mV for a ring of 8, 155 mV for 10, 111 mV for 14. Below that, if there is ATP to burn, it runs backwards, spending ATP to pump protons uphill. Real ones do this: many bacteria without oxygen keep their membranes charged this way.

The speed is a made-up but stated law, not a measurement: turns per second = 100 × tanh(excess / 10 kJ/mol), where the excess is (N/3) F Δp minus 50 kJ/mol. It is zero at the threshold, grows in proportion near it, and levels off at a hundred turns a second, roughly the fastest seen. ATP per second is three times that. The picture is slowed about a thousand times: at full speed one proton passes each second.

The felt balls are counted honestly. There are always 40 protons and 24 phosphate beads in the scene, and they only ever move. The free protons are shared between the two sides in the true ratio of their concentrations, ten to the power of the pH difference, as nearly as whole balls allow (never fewer than two a side). The purple lump at the left end stands for everything else that holds the gradient where you set it: in us, the chain of pumps that burns food; at a vent, the flow of fluid. It carries a ball back for each one that crosses. In the same way the cell spends each ATP as it is made, splitting it back on the green mats, so the dish holds four when ATP is plentiful and none when it is not.

What this leaves out

Nearly all the chemistry. The lobes do not really wait for three or four protons and then snap: the axle twists like a spring and smooths the mismatch between a ring of 8, 10 or 14 and a head of three. The price of ATP is held at 50 kJ/mol rather than worked out from concentrations. No protons leak or slip. The voltage and the pH difference count equally here; real turbines can respond to them differently. Mitochondria also spend about one more proton per ATP carrying phosphate and ADP in, and they have a small protein (IF1) that jams the turbine rather than let it run backwards. The gradient never sags, because the purple lump is not modelled at all. The adenosine that the phosphates hang on is not shown. And the head is drawn hanging down only so that the protons can fall.

Why it is in this lab

Bacteria, archaea and our own mitochondria all carry a rotary engine of this family, and all of them run it on a gradient of ions across a membrane. So the last common ancestor of all cells very probably did too; a reconstruction of its genes (M. Weiss and colleagues, Nature Microbiology 1, 2016) includes part of this machine. At alkaline hydrothermal vents a natural proton gradient of about the right size stands across thin mineral walls, with acid ocean on one side and alkaline fluid on the other. Some researchers, N. Lane and W. Martin among them, argue that life began by tapping it. Others doubt that a natural gradient could have been put to work before there were proteins like this one and membranes tight enough to hold it (J. B. Jackson, Journal of Molecular Evolution 83, 2016). Nobody knows. Press “Alkaline vent” to see that the numbers at least allow it: no voltage, three pH units, and with a ring of 10 or 14 the turbine turns.

The wonder

The real head is about 10 nanometres across, and the whole machine about 20 tall. It can turn a hundred times a second or more. A body holds only a fraction of a kilogram of ATP at any moment (estimates run from about 50 to 250 grams) but, by the usual estimate, makes and spends something like its own weight of it in a day, each molecule used and remade hundreds of times. The figure is worked out from how much food we burn, not weighed.
