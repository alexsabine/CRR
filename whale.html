What is known

The animal. The humpback, Megaptera novaeangliae, is a baleen whale of 12 to 16 metres. Its flippers are the longest of any whale, close to a third of its body length, with knobs called tubercles along the leading edge that keep water flowing over the flipper at steep angles (Fish and Battle 1995). There are more tubercles on the head, grooves along the throat that open like pleats when it feeds, a small fin on a hump, and broad flukes whose pale undersides differ from whale to whale (Clapham and Mead 1999).

The song. Payne and McVay (1971) found that the sounds are ordered. Single sounds, called units, make phrases. A phrase is repeated to make a theme. The themes come in the same order every time, and that is one song, 7 to 30 minutes long, sung again and again for hours. Only males sing, and all the males of one population sing the current version, which changes over the years and passes between populations (Garland and colleagues 2011). Baleen whales sing with the larynx, without letting air out (Elemans and colleagues 2024).

No echoes. Humpbacks do not echolocate. Finding prey by clicks and echoes belongs to the toothed whales: dolphins, porpoises, sperm whales. Nothing in this picture echolocates.

Feeding. Humpbacks trap fish with air. One or more whales circle beneath a school letting out bubbles, the rising curtain pens the fish, and the whales come up through the middle with mouths open (Jurasz and Jurasz 1979; Wiley and colleagues 2011). Herring will not cross a wall of bubbles (Sharpe and Dill 1997). A whale on its own can do it too (Szabo and colleagues 2024).

Breaching. A breaching humpback leaves the water at about 8 metres a second. It is among the most costly single movements any animal makes, and why they do it is not settled (Segre and colleagues 2020).

Sound and light in water

Sound travels faster in warmer water, in saltier water, and under more pressure. The panel uses Mackenzie’s (1981) nine-term equation. Going down from a warm surface the water cools and sound slows. Deeper still the temperature hardly changes but the pressure keeps rising, and sound speeds up again. Between the two is a depth where sound is slowest.

Sound bends towards slower water. A sound made near that depth keeps being turned back to it, so it spreads in a sheet, not a ball, and fades slowly. This is the deep sound channel (Ewing and Worzel 1948; Munk 1974). Payne and Webb (1971) worked out that the low calls of fin and blue whales could in principle carry across an ocean this way. In cold seas the slowest water is at the top and the channel comes up to the surface. Try the slider.

Humpbacks sing near the surface, far above the channel in warm seas, and their song is higher than a fin whale’s. It is heard for tens of kilometres, not thousands. Press the button in the panel to move the source between the singer and the channel.

Water takes the red out of sunlight within a few metres and lets blue through (Pope and Fry 1997), which is why everything at depth is blue and why the whale’s belly looks green-white, not white. The bright net moving on her back is sunlight focused by the waves.

The rule here

Each whale keeps a count that starts at zero at a breath and rises with its effort, faster when it swims hard. At a fixed total it comes up, blows, and the count starts again. The song is timed the same way: after a unit ends a count rises, and at a fixed total the next unit begins. The mother’s breaches come from a third count that rises by an uneven amount each second. The fish follow three local rules: do not crowd, steer with your neighbours, stay near them (Reynolds 1987; Couzin and colleagues 2002), and they flee what is large and close.

The timing rule here, a count since the last event, a fixed threshold and a restart, is that of CRR, a research programme under investigation. The whale biology and the acoustics are not CRR’s, and for the song the count is only a clock.

What this leaves out

Song belongs mostly to the winter breeding grounds and bubble nets to the cold summer feeding grounds. Here they share one sea, and the singer joins the net, which a singing male would not be doing. Dives are several times shorter than in life and the song lasts two minutes, not ten or twenty. The units were composed for this page after published descriptions. They are not a recording and not any real population’s song.

The whales do not hear each other, court, compete, or fear anything. The calf does not suckle. The fish have no hunger and are not replaced when eaten. There is no wind, swell, sea floor, or other life. The water does not move when a whale passes. The temperature profile is one smooth curve and the salt is the same at every depth; a real sea has layers, fronts and seasons. The rays ignore sound lost to the sea bed and to the water itself.
