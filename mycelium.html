What is known

A fungus is a mass of fine tubes, the hyphae. Each grows only at its tip. Parcels of wall material travel forward to a dense body just behind the apex, the Spitzenkörper, which hands them to the growing wall; the pressure of the cell pushes the new wall out (Bartnicki-Garcia, Hergert and Gierz 1989; Riquelme and others 2018).

A hypha branches when more material arrives than its tip can use (Trinci 1974; Prosser and Trinci 1979). Most branches leave the side a little behind the tip. Young tips keep away from one another. Older ones home in on neighbours and fuse, which turns a branching tree into a net with loops (Hickey and others 2002; Glass and others 2004).

Some wood-rotting fungi of the forest floor bundle their hyphae into cords and forage with them. From one piece of wood they spread thinly. When a cord finds new wood, the routes that join the two thicken and much of the rest is taken back and its contents reused (Boddy 1993, 1999; Bebber and others 2007). Thick cords are the ones that models of the flow say should carry most (Heaton and others 2010, 2012).

Fruit bodies are costly. They start when the mycelium has resources in hand, its growth is checked, and the conditions suit, above all enough water (Moore, Gange and Boddy 2008).

The rule used here

Flow. Each line is a strand, and a thick one stands for many hyphae side by side. Colonised wood pushes food in; growing tips, wood being colonised and young fruit bodies draw it out. A strand carries flow in proportion to the number of hyphae in it and the pressure difference along it. The pressures are solved over the whole net at every step.

Tips. A tip grows faster the more it is supplied, up to 8 mm a day. It turns away from crowded soil, towards wood within a centimetre or so, and wanders a little. A tip that is poorly supplied or hemmed in stops drawing.

Timing. The timing rule here is the same in five places: a count builds from a signal the fungus itself supplies, reaches a fixed threshold, something happens, and the count starts again from nothing. That is the timing grammar of the CRR programme, used here as a way of building the model and not as a finding. A tip counts its surplus supply and branches, to the side it has lately been drawn to. A tip close to another strand counts the closeness and fuses. A strand counts the flow beyond what it can carry and gains a hypha. A strand counts its shortfall and loses one, or is taken back and half its contents returned. Wood counts how full the net is in wet soil and starts a cluster of fruit bodies, where the cord with the strongest record of past flow joins it.

Checked before publishing. Twenty bets were written down before the model was first run. Fourteen of the nineteen about the model held on the first run, and fourteen, not the same ones, on the last; the bet about the page held. With fusion the net had 55 to 74 loops by day 12. New wood six centimetres off was met at once, since threads already lay under it; within ten days the route to it was a cord of 18 to 24 hyphae, and within fifteen 79 to 87% of the thread lying off that route had been taken back. A cut across the route dropped the join between the blocks to nothing or nearly, and new fusions closed the gap in every run. The spread of the time between branchings came out at 0.44 to 0.48 over about 400 intervals, three times the candidate value shown beside it. Five bets failed. With fusion switched off there were still one to three loops, made by tips growing back into their own wood. With no new wood, 61 to 73% of that same thread was taken back anyway, so the new wood added the ten points I bet on in only three runs of five. Eight days after the cut the join had recovered to between 16 and 58% of what it was; I bet half, and one run in five reached it. Economy did not rise by half from the first day to the twentieth; it peaked in between and ended a little lower in four runs of five. Branching per centimetre near the wood was 1.9 to 2.1 times that far out; I bet twice, and two runs in five reached it. After the first run I made new wood a stronger sink, because it had made no difference to what was taken back, and I mended a fault that let a cut-off fragment reach absurd pressures. Both changes came after seeing results, and the first run is kept.

What is left out

Scale. Real hyphae are a few thousandths of a millimetre across and a patch like this holds kilometres of them. The lines here are strands, and the enlarged tip is a drawing of a single hypha driven by one strand's numbers.

Chemistry. There is one resource, not carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus moving separately and sometimes in opposite directions. Water does not move, the soil is even, and there is no temperature, no season and no light.

Other life. No rival fungi, no bacteria, no grazing springtails, no tree roots.

Time and size. The patch is round, twenty centimetres across, and a tip that reaches its rim stops. A day passes in about two seconds. The fruit bodies are far smaller than life. The pull towards wood is weak and short, since the evidence that cords steer to wood from a distance is thin. The units of food are arbitrary.

Slime moulds are not fungi. The well-known result that a slime mould thickens the tubes that carry most flow (Tero and others 2010) is about Physarum; the rule here has the same form, and for fungi the support is Heaton and others (2010), who found thickening where their model put the largest currents. Growth rates, strand sizes and thresholds are typical or chosen values, not measurements of one species; the picture is loosely that of Phanerochaete velutina or Hypholoma fasciculare in a tray of soil. Other sources: Fricker, Heaton, Jones and Boddy (2017) on the mycelium as a network; Fleißner and others (2009) on the signalling between tips about to fuse. All references here are cited from memory and should be checked.
