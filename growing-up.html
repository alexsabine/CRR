Growing Up, Twice

Piaget and Erikson are often taught side by side as if they were rival maps. They are maps of different things.

0years old

Jean Piaget

One staircase

What kind of thinking is possible. Each step replaces the last.

Erik Erikson

Eight strands

How a person stands towards others and themselves. Nothing is replaced; each strand has its season, and all of them run the whole way.

The difference

Piaget describes a sequence of reorganisations. The child takes the world in with the thinking it has (assimilation). What does not fit builds up. At some point the thinking itself gives way and is rebuilt (accommodation). Then the child is on a new step and does not go back.

Erikson describes eight tensions, each between two poles. Each comes to the front at a certain age, is settled more or less well, and stays with the person. Later strands are coloured by how earlier ones went.

Try this

Move Things to handle. The steps on the left arrive sooner or later. The strands on the right do not move.

Move Being held. The strands lean one way or the other. The staircase does not move.

That is what it means to say they lie on different axes. One is about what a mind can do. The other is about how a person stands.

What is running

Left. A count of what does not fit builds up with age and with things to handle. At a threshold it drops to zero and the child steps up. That is CRR used as a rule of thumb. The model is tuned so that, at the default setting, the steps land on Piaget’s ages (about 2, 7 and 11).

Right. Each strand settles during its season from how well the child is held, nudged by the average of the strands before it. Ages are Erikson’s.

Left out. In life the two lean on each other: a child who is held explores more. They are kept apart here so the difference can be seen. Stage ages vary between children and cultures, and both theories have well-known critics. This is a teaching picture, not evidence for either theory or for CRR. An earlier version is kept.
