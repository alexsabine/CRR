What is known

A young leaf makes the hormone auxin all over its blade. Veins form where the auxin drains, and each new vein drains its neighbourhood, so the next one starts further off (Sachs 1981). Runions and colleagues (2005) turned this into a rule that draws convincing leaves.

A surface catches light in proportion to the cosine of the angle between the beam and its normal (Lambert 1760). Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red strongly and green weakly. A leaf still takes about three quarters of the green, but what leaves it is mostly green, so that is what we see.

Photosystem II splits water one photon at a time. Its manganese centre steps through four states and releases one molecule of oxygen on the fourth step (Joliot 1969; Kok, Forbush and McGloin 1970). In the dark most centres rest on the second state, so the first burst comes on the third flash.

In autumn chlorophyll is taken apart and its nitrogen withdrawn. The yellow carotenoids were there all summer and now show. The red anthocyanins are newly made, most in bright light with cold nights (Feild, Lee and Holbrook 2001; Hoch, Zeldin and McCown 2001).

The rule used here

Veins. The open-venation algorithm of Runions et al. Auxin sources are scattered in a growing blade. Each pulls the nearest vein towards it and is removed when a vein arrives. Widths follow Murray’s law, the cube of a vein being the sum of the cubes of its branches.

Light. Direct sun, weakened and reddened by the air it crosses (Kasten and Young 1989 air mass, Rayleigh and haze). Cosine law at the blade. Fresnel reflection at the cuticle (Schlick 1994). Inside, a two-flux model of an absorbing, scattering layer (Kubelka and Munk 1931), which reduces to Beer and Lambert’s law without scattering. Colour on screen is worked out from the spectrum of light leaving the leaf, from the amounts of four pigments.

Counting. Twelve thousand model centres. Half the photons caught by chlorophyll and carotenoids go to photosystem II. A centre that has just used a photon is busy for about ten milliseconds and wastes any that arrive meanwhile. One hit in ten is a miss. The timing rule here is count, threshold, restart: each centre counts photons, lets go of oxygen on the fourth, and begins again. That is the Kok cycle, which is established biology; it has the shape the CRR programme studies, and no more is claimed.

Checked before publishing. Twenty-five bets were written down first; twenty-two held. From rest in the dark, oxygen peaked on flash 3 and again on flash 7 in five runs of five, and matched the exact four-state chain. Photons absorbed followed the cosine of the angle within 4% up to 60°, and fell 39% short at 80°, where the cuticle reflects. Three bets failed. I bet flash 11 would stand above its neighbours; flashes 11 and 12 came out almost level. I bet that doubling full-strength light would add under 15% oxygen; it added 16%. I bet at least 0.100 oxygen per absorbed photon in weak light; the run gave 0.0985, about ten photons for each molecule. The first pigment curves also absorbed too much (0.90 against a bet of 0.78 to 0.90) and gave too narrow a green; I widened the green window once, after seeing that, and the figure is now 0.87.

What is left out

Vein loops. Real leaves of this kind have a closed net; this one is a branching tree, with the finest mesh drawn as texture. The patchiness of autumn colour is drawn, not modelled.

Stomata, carbon dioxide, the Calvin cycle, respiration, water and heat. The oxygen figure is gross, and its ceiling is set only by how fast a centre can turn over. Photoinhibition and protective quenching are absent, and every centre sees the same light, which is not true through the thickness of a real leaf.

Skylight. The numbers use the direct beam only, so an edge-on blade reads zero.

Time. The rings run four hundred times slower than life. The leaf answers the sun in seconds, where turning takes minutes to hours and reddening takes days. Flashes in the original experiments were a third of a second apart.

Pigment absorption curves are smooth approximations set to give a typical green leaf, not measured spectra; the standard model is PROSPECT (Jacquemoud and Baret 1990). Figures for pigment amounts, centres per chlorophyll and turnover time are typical values. Colour matching uses the fits of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley (2013).
